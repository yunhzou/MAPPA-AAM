\section{Introduction}

Atom-to-atom mapping identifies corresponding atoms across a chemical reaction. These correspondences support reaction-center identification, template extraction and the preparation of structures for reaction-path calculations. Yet endpoint structures do not always determine a unique assignment. Different correspondences may imply different atom origins and bond changes, while molecular symmetry can produce many equivalent assignments. A useful mapping method should retain structural alternatives without repeatedly listing permutations of the same choice.

Existing approaches obtain correspondences from learned reaction representations or graph-based optimization. RXNMapper uses attention from a reaction language model~\citep{schwaller2021}, LocalMapper learns from chemist-labeled reactions~\citep{chen2024}, and SLAPMapper combines label refinement with sequential linear assignment~\citep{koda2025}. For methods returning alternatives, recovering a reference somewhere in the output is a different objective from predicting one correct top-ranked mapping. Symmetry also makes the number of explicit assignments a poor measure of the number of structural choices.

Weighted molecular graphs introduce another distinction. Two assignments can both satisfy a permissive bond-weight matching tolerance while producing different bond events at a finer threshold. Retaining one representative alone can therefore hide information needed for chemical interpretation. The search for correspondences and their interpretation as bond changes should remain separate.

MAPPA separates three decisions: whether a fragment can continue growing, which compressed placements its completed constraints permit, and what bond changes those placements imply. Growth retains one compatible assignment. At saturation, a separate operation constructs the distinct compressed fragment placements, which become conditional branches for mapping the remaining atoms. A cut sweep varies the growth conditions to expose alternatives excluded by greedy fragment commitment. The completed search is reduced to a catalogue of mapping families, and a separate decoder shares local bond-event calculations across its unique fragments. We describe these stages and evaluate reference recovery and computational cost on Golden~\citep{lin2022} and EnzymeMap~\citep{heid2023enzymemap}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Scientific stages and module boundaries}
\label{sec:architecture}

MAPPA separates correspondence discovery from bond-event interpretation. Matching returns conditional fragment placements. Reduction collects them into a catalogue shared across seeds and cuts. Decoding prepares reusable fragment responses and returns branches that can be queried within an event-count window (\Cref{fig:algorithm}). These stages have separate inputs and outputs: decoding a saved catalogue does not repeat matching.

A \emph{fragment} pairs a source atom set with a target atom set and retains one original discovery record. A \emph{witness} is one explicit correspondence permitted by that record. A \emph{branch} is a composition of fragment choices; a \emph{mapping family} retains their selected local relations and ordered placement context. Symmetry describes correlated rearrangements within these records, rather than a list of complete atom permutations.

The implementation follows these scientific boundaries. A shared core supplies binary symmetry, constrained assignment and event-response operations. Matching, reduction and decoding consume that core through distinct stage interfaces. Execution and persistence manage work and intermediate results; they do not decide which mappings are allowed. Reference verification is an external evaluation of the saved outputs (\Cref{sec:verification}).

\subsection{Endpoint graphs and discovery constraints}
\label{sec:matching-rules}

Let $V_R,V_P$ be the endpoint atom sets, $Z_R,Z_P$ their elements and $W_R,W_P$ their bond-weight matrices. A partial mapping $m:D\subseteq V_R\rightarrow V_P$ is injective and element-preserving. Explicit hydrogens participate. Coordinates may accompany the inputs, but do not determine growth.

The active source graph contains bonds at or above the presence threshold $b$. A growing fragment stores only its accepted preservation edges $A$. For each $(u,v)\in A$, discovery requires
\begin{equation}
 Z_R(u)=Z_P(m(u)),\quad W_P(m(u),m(v))\geq b,\quad
 |W_R(u,v)-W_P(m(u),m(v))|\leq\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}.
 \label{eq:match}
\end{equation}
Previously completed assignments remain locked during subsequent growth. Other bonds between included atoms are not implicitly added as constraints, and source nonedges impose no negative constraint. The complete original matrices are retained for event interpretation. Thus the accepted growth graph can differ from the fragment's final internal connectivity.

\subsection{One-witness fragment growth}
\label{sec:growth}

Starting from a seed, MAPPA selects a boundary atom uniformly and then one of its incident frontier edges uniformly. Each proposal asks a single question: does the enlarged fragment have at least one compatible injective assignment? Bond weight does not rank proposals.

The current witness is extended when possible. Otherwise, a previously discovered fragment can certify existence under the current occupancy and edge constraints (\Cref{sec:reuse-execution}). If neither supplies a witness, the existence solver propagates element, bond-support and distinct-target constraints before searching the remaining choices. Assignments within the growing fragment may change; completed-fragment locks remain fixed. The query stops at the first witness. Only exhaustion establishes absence, so a resource limit cannot become a rejected chemical match.

A successful proposal adds its edge and replaces the witness. An impossible proposal is deferred while other frontier edges are considered. When a completed fragment is joined, its locked assignments and accepted edges are retained. Saturation occurs when no admissible extension remains. Intermediate growth therefore does not enumerate all matches of each shorter fragment.

\subsection{Compressed completion and the two roles of symmetry}
\label{sec:completion}

At saturation, a separate operation discovers the allowed target atom sets for the completed source fragment. It uses the full accepted constraints and exclusions for placements already represented, rather than emitting every internal bijection and merging duplicates. A six-membered carbon ring mapped onto one equivalent ring gives one atom-set placement; two rings with distinct surrounding environments can give two placements. Their internal rotations do not create additional atom-set candidates.

Symmetry has two roles. \emph{Fragment-local symmetry} is computed on each fragment's own binary internal graph, preserving elements and fixing its anchors. Bonds above the presence criterion have one structural label; original weights remain available for event counting. Nauty provides compact generators~\citep{mckay2014}, each describing a permitted joint rearrangement. For example, exchanging two identical arms moves their attached atoms together; it does not allow independent atom choices that break connectivity. Both source and target fragment actions are retained. If an aligned pair has equal binary connectivity, one side suffices to represent their relative rearrangements.

\emph{Global placement symmetry} acts on an entire endpoint and can connect different target atom sets. To share a subsequent search continuation, MAPPA uses only a certified subgroup that preserves the target weights, active connectivity, source-to-target compatibility and earlier locks. The saved action moves the whole continuation jointly. This stronger requirement protects later growth; it is distinct from the permissive local binary symmetry used in decoding. If global equivalence is broken by external attachments, the local fragment can still have its own symmetry.

A fragment stores its atom-set pair, original witness, discovery constraints, anchors and local symmetry relations. Different discovery correspondences on the same sets need not belong to one binary-symmetry orbit. This distinction matters when decoding selects a starting correspondence (\Cref{sec:decoding}); neither a single witness nor individual atom domains alone describe every correlated choice.

\subsection{Hierarchical branching, pruning and cut sweep}
\label{sec:cut-sweep}

Each completed placement creates a continuation for the remaining atoms. Earlier choices condition later ones (\Cref{fig:algorithm}a), and targets cannot be occupied twice. After each finished search step, the hierarchy is recounted from its leaves upward. At an over-limit node, the largest child subtree is removed, the node is recounted, and pruning continues until its retained count is within the branch cap. An ancestor uses the already-reduced descendant count. The cap counts representative continuations, not the symmetry-related bijections within a fragment. Truncation remains explicit.

Greedy growth can commit to constraints that exclude another useful correspondence. MAPPA varies these commitments through independent seed orderings and a single-edge cut sweep. For $G_R=(V_R,E_R)$, the sweep searches the uncut graph and $G_R^{(e)}=(V_R,E_R\setminus\{e\})$ for each eligible edge, including bonds to hydrogen. Each seed setting supplies that many source-atom orderings per cut condition.

A masked edge changes growth, not the original endpoint weights. Its endpoints may reconnect through another route, and the final mapping may preserve that bond. Conversely, one mask can redirect several later choices and expose multiple bond changes. Both reaction directions are searched and retained in their own atom-index frames. Seeds, cuts and bidirectional search broaden exploration, but do not make it exhaustive.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Matching, reduction and separate decoding}
\label{alg:mappa-overview}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Endpoint graphs, discovery policies, seed/cut plan, branch cap and event window
\FORALL{requested search directions}
  \FORALL{cut conditions and seed orderings}
    \STATE Initialize the conditional search hierarchy
    \WHILE{a retained continuation has an eligible fragment seed}
      \STATE Grow using one-witness existence queries
      \STATE At saturation, construct distinct compressed placements
      \STATE Continue each placement with earlier assignments locked
      \STATE Prune and recount the hierarchy from leaves to root
    \ENDWHILE
    \STATE Retain terminal compositions and compact provenance
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE Share fragments by atom-set pair; reduce repeated branch compositions
\ENDFOR
\STATE Complete the matching/reduction stage before batch decoding
\FORALL{reaction/direction catalogues}
  \STATE Inventory unique fragments and prepare their shared symmetry-event responses
  \STATE Compose branch contributions and the sweep-endpoint correction
  \STATE Retain queryable branches within the requested event window
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Atom-set reduction and the mapping catalogue}
\label{sec:reduction}

Reduction identifies each completed fragment by the pair $(R_i,P_i)$ of source and target atom sets. It retains one original fragment record per pair and gives it a shared identifier. A final branch is grouped by its unordered composition
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal B=\{(R_1,P_1),\ldots,(R_q,P_q)\}.
 \label{eq:branch}
\end{equation}
Repeated witnesses or growth orderings do not create new fragment identities. Branch compositions also retain their ordered global placement context: overlapping actions must remain correlated, whereas independent decisions may be reordered.

This is a structural aggregation. It reuses the inventories accumulated during matching, translates compositions to shared fragment identifiers and deduplicates those compositions. It performs no symmetry solving, starting-map optimization or event counting. The retained original record keeps its witness, domains, accepted edges and anchors intact. Duplicate discovery records are discarded rather than combined into a broader relation. Consequently, reduction selects a representative discovery record; it does not preserve the union of every discarded starting relation. Alternative partitions and cut histories remain compact provenance for interpreting the retained families. One complete reaction/direction catalogue, rather than an isolated cut, is the input to decoding.

\subsection{Shared fragment symmetry-to-event responses}
\label{sec:decoding}

The decoder inventories the unique fragments before preparing their responses. Events use the original endpoint weights:
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{uv}(m)=W_P(m(u),m(v))-W_R(u,v).
 \label{eq:events}
\end{equation}
A change is counted when $|\Delta_{uv}|$ is strictly greater than $0.5$, or $0.3$ for a pair involving a metal. Signs distinguish formation or strengthening from cleavage or weakening. These event thresholds are separate from discovery tolerance; binary rearrangements can preserve connectivity while changing weighted bond events.

For each unique fragment, decoding retains one starting correspondence and its permitted local binary symmetries. It first improves the saved witness under those symmetries. Additional starting-correspondence search is limited to fragments whose accepted growth connectivity differs from their final connectivity on at least one side, and whose current witness remains above an established lower bound. This work has a fixed per-fragment budget. An attained bound certifies the minimum within its stated scope; otherwise the result is the best witness found with incomplete optimality. Selecting one starting orbit is an explicit restriction on decoded coverage.

The shared response contains correlated symmetry operations, a fixed contribution and sparse variable bond-event terms. Independent components are separated only when neither an event term nor a requested endpoint couples them. Event-invariant operations can share computation. Occupancy remains joint: two arms cannot choose the same target position. The decoder retains this compact relation instead of materializing every permutation or every event total. A later query asks for an assignment at a selected count or with selected atom pairs and returns a replayable witness, absence or an unresolved outcome.

\subsection{Branch composition, sweep correction and event windows}
\label{sec:window}

Each branch references the prepared local responses. Its reported count uses the independent ordinary-boundary model
\begin{equation}
 E_{\mathcal B}(m)=B_{\mathcal B}+\sum_i e_i(m)+s_{\mathcal B}(m),
 \qquad 0\leq s_{\mathcal B}(m)\leq 2.
 \label{eq:composition}
\end{equation}
Here $e_i$ counts internal changes owned by a disjoint final fragment piece, $B_{\mathcal B}$ is the fixed ordinary-boundary contribution, and $s_{\mathcal B}$ is the correction associated with the single swept bond. Mapped/unmapped boundaries contribute without inventing correspondences for unmatched atoms; bonds wholly within unmatched material are omitted. Alternative partitions or cut histories are alternative compositions, not additive contributions.

The sweep correction depends on its two endpoints and on local choices that move them. Assembly reuses the prepared fragment responses and preserves the joint endpoint/internal-event relationship. Shared placement actions remain one choice across the continuation. Ordinary boundaries, however, are not recomputed for every rearrangement. Equation~\eqref{eq:composition} is therefore an approximation to the physical whole-reaction event count, not a claim that all inter-fragment changes are independent.

Window selection combines local lower bounds with attained branch witnesses. If attained local minima compose, the smallest pre-sweep composition $L$ has an attained upper bound at most $L+2$. Only compositions that can improve the current best count need the corresponding refinement. If local optimization is incomplete or its witnesses cannot compose, the decoder instead uses the honest lower bound and an actual attained upper bound $U$; $L+2$ is then not assumed to be attainable. Branches whose lower bounds exceed $U+n$ are excluded, and the survivors retain their event-query responses. The studies below use $n=5$. A proved model minimum, a best-found model count and a physical event minimum remain distinct.

\subsection{Fragment reuse and portable execution}
\label{sec:reuse-execution}

Matching accumulates known fragments during search. For example, a stored match $R\{1,2,3,4\}\rightarrow P\{a,b,c,d\}$ can certify a later proposal on $R\{2,3\}$ when its restricted witness satisfies the proposed edges, existing locks and current target availability. An indexed containment check retrieves such a proof without searching its symmetry assignments. A miss leaves the ordinary existence query to decide; it does not establish absence. Every seed and cut still makes fresh growth decisions, and completion chooses its representatives independently of the temporary cached witness.

The same stage interfaces support direct local calls and persistent worker pools. A pool is created once and schedules reaction, seed or cut work without putting scheduler decisions inside the algorithms. For a large campaign, all matching and reduction finish before decoding begins. Intermediate records can remain in memory or pass through aggregated storage with shared immutable fragments; file counts do not grow with individual seeds or cuts. Compatible reaction-cache snapshots share useful fragment proofs across workers. Retention limits affect reuse, not the declared mapping relation.

Bulk assignment, composition and event operations use native kernels, while the public records and stage interfaces remain in Python. The shared symmetry/event core serves both local fragment preparation and branch decoding. Persistence stores the compact responses and chosen starting correspondences, so loading a decoded result does not repeat optimization. Detailed query, storage and cache rules appear in the supplement.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Datasets and search settings}
\label{sec:current-validation}

We evaluate all 1,851 Golden reactions~\citep{lin2022} and the prepared collection of 47,974 EnzymeMap reactions~\citep{heid2023enzymemap}. Reference labels are removed before search. Both studies use the prepared formal-bond-order matrices and explicit hydrogens; these results do not constitute a benchmark of continuously valued electronic-structure WBO inputs. EnzymeMap references are curated database mappings, so agreement is not independent validation of reaction mechanisms.

The common settings are discovery tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}=2$, presence/cut threshold $b=0.2$, hierarchical branch cap 100, random seed 42, both directions and the full single-edge sweep. Golden uses one and ten seed orderings per cut; EnzymeMap uses one. Decoding uses the original weights, thresholds 0.5/0.3, a five-second allowance per unique fragment and the best attained model count plus five. No reference mapping guides growth, pruning or starting-correspondence selection.

\subsection{Catalogue and decoded-reference verification}
\label{sec:verification}

We report two external coverage measurements. \emph{Catalogue recovery} asks whether the reference belongs to the retained matching relation. \emph{Decoded recovery} requires an actual injective mapping from a saved starting correspondence and its joint symmetry choices, in a retained branch and event window. A positive certificate is replayed and its event count independently checked. A catalogue certificate may suggest a candidate, but cannot alone establish decoded membership.

The decoded audits preserve exact annotated heavy-atom indices and unmatched-heavy-atom requirements. Unannotated hydrogens must admit a feasible completion. Golden catalogue verification additionally permits endpoint chemical symmetries preserving charge, isotope, hydrogen count and stereochemistry; the Golden catalogue and decoded scores therefore differ in both representation and reference-equivalence policy. EnzymeMap uses exact indices for both. Recovery means inclusion in either search direction, not top-ranked prediction accuracy. Every reaction remains in the denominator, and unresolved queries remain separate from absence.

\subsection{Timing and branch-event analysis}
\label{sec:measurement}

Mapping time is the sum of matching and reduction; decoding is reported separately. CPU seconds sum successful worker computation over both directions and all cuts, including stage input loading and result construction. Publication, external verification, historical failed attempts and parallel elapsed duration have separate accounting. Timings use the saved successful passes and their documented recoveries, rather than a newly measured uninterrupted run of one final performance revision. Exact source and measurement boundaries are recorded in the repository.

To locate a recovered reference within the decoded event landscape, let $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{GT}}$ contain the retained branch contexts that admit it inside the saved window. We measure
\begin{equation}
 n_{\mathrm{GT}}=\min_{c\in\mathcal C_{\mathrm{GT}}}\min_{m\in c} E_c(m)-U,
 \label{eq:reference-offset}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the overall detected count, minimized across both directions. Ground truth selects the qualifying contexts; their event minima are queried without fixing the reference assignments. Both terms use the same saved all-atom decoder model. This measures where a GT-containing branch begins, not the physical event count of the reference itself. Missing references and unresolved offsets remain outside numeric bins.

\subsection{Seed scaling and fragment reuse}

Increasing Golden from one to ten seeds increases matching CPU by 9.42-fold, reduction by 8.09-fold and decoding by 4.95-fold. Decoding therefore grows less than seed count in this comparison, consistent with sharing fragment work across the reduced output. These ratios compare different retained workloads and do not isolate a cache speedup. In particular, matching remains close to linear in the number of seed orderings.

The full Golden seed comparison predates the current indexed fragment-containment cache. We assess that cache separately on three selected reactions, in both directions, using an Apple M4 Pro CPU. With one seed, the median matching CPU sum is 0.678 seconds with retention versus 1.008 without; with ten seeds it is 7.075 versus 10.477 seconds. The approximately 1.48-fold benefit includes immutable-record sharing as well as existence-query reuse. All 48 measured direction runs retain identical catalogue fingerprints, search scope and cap outcomes. These controls support useful reuse, but do not establish a dataset-wide cache speedup or strongly sublinear matching.

\section{Discussion}

MAPPA separates the existence question needed during growth from the compressed alternatives needed at saturation, and then separates correspondence discovery from event interpretation. Atom-set reduction gives repeated fragments one shared identity. The decoder reuses local symmetry-event responses while retaining correlated occupancy and the special dependence introduced by a swept bond. These module boundaries make search, representation and interpretation independently inspectable and reusable.

The benchmarks demonstrate high reference inclusion with complete saved mapping and decoding outputs, while preserving the distinction between catalogue and decoded coverage. More Golden seeds recover additional references at increased computation. Fragment reuse reduces repeated work, but the measured matching cost remains approximately proportional to seed count. This evidence supports reporting stage-specific costs rather than treating a high cache hit rate as proof of a large overall speedup.

Several limits remain scientifically important. Greedy trajectories, sampled seeds, single-edge cuts and hierarchical caps restrict discovery. Selecting one original fragment record per atom-set pair and one decoded starting orbit can omit alternatives present in discarded discovery records. Bounded improvement can retain an unproved minimum. None of these restrictions establishes exhaustive correspondence recovery.

The fixed ordinary-boundary model is also an approximation. In the EnzymeMap audit, 2,801 positive directional witnesses have different model and physical whole-reaction event counts; the ten-seed Golden audit records 32 such witnesses. Both counts are retained. Model-window membership is therefore not physical event-bin accuracy, and a low model count alone does not identify the annotated or most plausible chemistry. The branch-offset figure assesses organization within that model, not a replacement accuracy measure.

Retained alternatives can support reaction-path preparation: atom-index assignment affects endpoint interpolation~\citep{de2019}, and different permitted correspondences could supply starting structures for nudged elastic band calculations~\citep{henkelman2000}. These are downstream uses, not demonstrated improvements in transition-state prediction. The current Golden and EnzymeMap studies also do not validate continuously valued WBO inputs or every stereochemical attribute. The archived comparator, seed/cap, direction and coordinate analyses remain in the supplement with their original protocols; they do not establish a current-version speed ranking.
