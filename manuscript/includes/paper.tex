\section{Introduction}

Atom-to-atom mapping (AAM) identifies corresponding atoms between two molecular endpoints, each of which may contain several molecules. For a chosen search direction, we call one endpoint the \emph{base} and the other the \emph{target}. Either reaction side can take either role; reversing the search exchanges them. A mapping preserves element identity and uses each mapped atom only once. Comparing bonds between corresponding atom pairs then reveals the net changes between the endpoints: a bond present in the base but absent in the target is a cleavage in that orientation, and a formation in the reverse orientation. AAM describes the endpoint correspondence of chemical evolution; the intervening sequence of elementary steps requires additional information.

These assignments make reactions usable as structured data. They locate reaction centers and enable the extraction of transformation templates for reaction prediction and retrosynthesis~\citep{schwaller2021,chen2024}. They also establish atom identities between endpoint geometries for reaction-path calculations~\citep{de2019}. A mistaken correspondence can change the inferred transformation and propagate into downstream analyses. MAPPA addresses this task by exploring shared molecular fragments and the alternative mappings they support (\Cref{fig:algorithm}).

The difficulty is that endpoint structures alone may not select a unique correspondence. Repeated groups offer several possible matching sites, and molecular symmetry permits exchanges that leave a structure unchanged. Other assignments can imply different atom origins or different bond changes. For example, when several molecules at one endpoint contain oxygen, deciding which oxygen corresponds to a particular site at the other endpoint may require chemical knowledge beyond element identity. Favoring a small number of bond changes is a useful structural preference, but does not by itself establish the chemically correct atom history~\citep{chen2024}. A mapper must therefore balance the cost of exploring alternatives with the risk of discarding a useful one.

Golden case 9 makes this ambiguity concrete (\Cref{fig:alternatives}). The annotated correspondence places the alcohol oxygen in the ring at the opposite endpoint and the ketone oxygen in water. A second assignment exchanges those oxygen origins, while a third also changes two carbon assignments. The second has fewer bond events than the reference; the first and third have the same total but different atom origins. A minimum-event rule alone therefore does not select the annotation. Retaining alternatives leaves that chemical decision available to a subsequent selector.

Existing approaches use structural optimization or information learned from reaction data to guide these assignments. Structural methods seek shared substructures or favorable bond-change patterns~\citep{lin2022}; SLAPMapper uses iterative atom-label refinement and assignment optimization~\citep{koda2025}. RXNMapper obtains correspondence information from a reaction language model~\citep{schwaller2021}, while LocalMapper learns from chemist-labeled examples~\citep{chen2024}. For the present work, a further distinction is useful: whether the intended output is a set of alternatives or one selected prediction (\Cref{tab:aam-objectives}). This distinction concerns the answer supplied to the user and can apply to either structural or learned methods.

The two objectives can be combined in a discovery-and-selection workflow. An alternative-generating solver supplies candidates; a selector then chooses among them using a preference such as fewest bond events, chemical rules or a learned ranking. Coverage measures whether the reference remains available for that later choice, whereas top-1 accuracy measures whether the choice is correct. All methods can be compared by reference coverage: a single prediction is a candidate set containing one mapping. Coverage of a larger set does not establish equally high top-1 accuracy after selection. MAPPA focuses on the discovery step and retains alternatives for subsequent interpretation and selection.

The central challenge for this objective is to preserve useful diversity without repeatedly processing equivalent atom assignments. MAPPA treats mapping as a constraint-search problem: shared molecular fragments restrict which atoms can correspond, while compact symmetry relationships retain allowed rearrangements. The matching stage discovers these fragments and their combinations, then consolidates repeated fragments and branch compositions. The decoding stage relates the retained alternatives to bond changes. This separation allows fragment calculations to be reused across alternatives and supports parallel exploration. We evaluate reference coverage, computational cost and the choices remaining under a minimum-bond-event preference on Golden~\citep{lin2022} and EnzymeMap~\citep{heid2023enzymemap}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Overview: from shared fragments to alternative mappings}
\label{sec:architecture}
\label{sec:design-principle}

MAPPA builds atom mappings by growing structural fragments shared between the base and target endpoints (\Cref{fig:algorithm}). Starting from a seed atom, it extends a fragment while a compatible placement remains possible. When no admissible extension remains, it finds the completed fragment's alternative placements. Each placement defines a search branch: that choice is locked, and fragment growth continues on the remaining atoms under the accumulated assignments. Repeating this process produces alternative compositions of matched fragments. Different seeds, single-bond cuts and search directions expose additional compositions.

Two stages organize this procedure. The \emph{matching stage} discovers fragments and their conditional placements, then reduces repeated fragments and branch compositions into a shared mapping catalogue. Reduction is its final subphase. The \emph{decoding stage} uses that catalogue to relate permitted atom rearrangements to bond events, preparing each unique fragment once and combining its response with the branch boundaries. Its output retains branches that can be queried within a chosen event-count window, rather than only one selected mapping. Matching and decoding are separate operations; a saved catalogue can be decoded without repeating discovery. Reference verification evaluates these outputs separately (\Cref{sec:verification}).

\subsection{Matching Stage: grow, saturate and branch}
\label{sec:matching-rules}

Matching alternates between \emph{growth matching}, which tests whether an enlarged fragment has any placement, and \emph{saturation matching}, which finds the finished fragment's distinct placements. Postponing branching lets a small, common motif grow into a larger, more restrictive constraint (\Cref{fig:algorithm}a).

\subsubsection{Growth matching: keep extending while a match exists}
\label{sec:growth}

The growing fragment $g=(B_g,A_g)$ contains base atoms $B_g$ and accepted edges $A_g$; $\mathcal L$ records earlier locked assignments. A \emph{witness} $m$ maps these atoms injectively into the target, preserving elements and locks, with each accepted edge placed on a target bond within tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}$. Explicit hydrogens participate. Other internal bonds and base nonedges impose no additional matching requirement.

Growth starts from a seed with an available target atom. The presence threshold $b$ defines eligible connections. It selects a boundary atom uniformly, then one of its frontier edges uniformly, and tentatively forms $g^+$. The decision is an existence test:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Exists}(g^+\mid\mathcal L)
 =\mathbf{1}\!\left[\mathcal M(g^+\mid\mathcal L)\ne\varnothing\right],
 \label{eq:growth-exists}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal M$ is the set of compatible injective maps. If a witness is found, accept the extension. If no placement exists, defer that edge, keep $g$, and try the remaining frontier. \emph{Saturation occurs only when no eligible extension remains.} One failed edge does not end growth.

Let $\nu(g)$ count distinct target atom sets admitting a placement (\Cref{tab:growth-transitions}). Growth does not compute this count: every positive outcome means continue growing, regardless of multiplicity.

For $1\to\mathrm{many}$, consider one eligible target carbon with two oxygen neighbors. C has one target atom set; C--O has two. Finding either placement accepts the extension without branching. The witness can change target sites during later growth, while earlier locks remain fixed. Extending the current witness or reusing a compatible saved fragment can avoid a new search; otherwise the existence solver finds one witness or establishes absence. A resource limit is not a zero-match answer.

\subsubsection{Saturation matching: recover distinct atom-set placements}
\label{sec:completion}

At saturation, MAPPA finds distinct target atom sets $T_i=m(B_g)$. Each pair $(B_g,T_i)$ defines a completed fragment placement, with its internal mapping relation and fragment-local binary symmetries retained compactly.

A six-carbon ring fragment fitting two target rings with different external attachments gives two atom-set placements. Rotations and reflections within either ring belong to that placement. Search excludes already represented target sets directly, avoiding enumeration of their bijections followed by deduplication.

Fragment-local symmetry describes rearrangements \emph{within} a placement. Certified global symmetry can share work \emph{between} placements, but only if it preserves the entire remaining matching problem, moving later fragments jointly and respecting earlier locks. Otherwise those placements remain separate (\Cref{sec:completion-details}).

\subsubsection{Hierarchical branching: lock a placement and continue}
\label{sec:branching}

Each completed placement creates a child continuation: lock its assignments and grow the next fragment. In \Cref{fig:algorithm}a, choosing A$\to a$ leaves sites $b,c$ for B; choosing B$\to b$ leaves $c$ for C. These are conditional decisions: two fragments cannot independently occupy the same target atoms. Joining an earlier fragment retains its locked assignments and accepted constraints.

Each locked fragment expresses a shared structural constraint, extended as far as the current greedy trajectory permits. Another seed may discover a different constraint. After each completed step, apply the branch cap from leaves to root: discard the largest sibling subtrees as needed, recount, then assess the parent. Internal symmetry permutations do not count as branches. Caps restrict coverage and are recorded explicitly (\Cref{sec:hierarchy-details}).

\subsection{Matching Stage: seeds, cuts and search direction}
\label{sec:cut-sweep}

\textbf{Vary the starting point.} One greedy trajectory can repeatedly favor a large preserved region and miss another useful decomposition. In \Cref{fig:design-examples}a, growing the large B--A region first tends to leave R--B as the next fragment. If B--A contains 90\% of the eligible seed atoms, a uniformly chosen initial seed falls there with probability 0.9. Additional seeds can discover different constraints, but may also revisit the dominant decomposition.
\par\end{samepage}

\textbf{Vary the allowed connections.} A single-edge \emph{cut sweep} perturbs this commitment systematically. MAPPA searches the uncut graph and then repeats the search with each eligible base edge temporarily masked, including bonds to hydrogen. Each cut condition receives the requested number of seed orderings. Masking a connection inside the dominant region can expose a trajectory that ordinary seeding rarely reaches. The original endpoint weights remain unchanged for decoding: a mask alters discovery and does not assert a broken bond. Its endpoints may reconnect through another route, and one cut can redirect several later fragment choices.

\textbf{Exchange the endpoints.} Exchanging the base and target adds further alternatives. Seeds, cuts and reverse searches broaden discovery without making it exhaustive. The same exploration principle can be applied to another mapper; our expanded SLAP protocol reaches approximately 97\% coverage, although its changes in both search scope and weight mode prevent a sweep-only attribution (\Cref{sec:paired-slap}).

\subsection{Matching Stage: consolidate unique fragments and branches}
\label{sec:reduction}

Different seeds and cuts often rediscover the same fragment. The final matching subphase, \emph{reduction}, gives each base/target atom-set pair $(B_i,T_i)$ one shared identity $F_i$. For example, two searches that match $\{1,2,3\}$ to $\{a,b,c\}$ reference the same $F_i$, even if their growth order or starting witness differs. Each branch then references its fragment identities, allowing repeated compositions to share one catalogue entry.

A fragment identity is a key, not permission to map its atoms arbitrarily. Reduction keeps one original record with its witness, discovery constraints, anchors and symmetry relation. Branches also retain the ordered placement context needed to move later fragments consistently. Equal compositions are consolidated with that context preserved; their cut provenance is collected together. Selecting one original record does not retain the union of all discarded discovery relations.

This subphase reuses the fragment inventories already accumulated during matching. It performs structural grouping, with no symmetry optimization or event computation. Its output is one shared catalogue per reaction and direction. That catalogue is the input to decoding.

\subsection{Decoding Stage: prepare each fragment's symmetry--event response}
\label{sec:decoding}

Decoding turns the retained mapping alternatives into queryable bond-event information. It first prepares each unique fragment once, then reuses that response wherever the fragment occurs. The local calculation connects \emph{which atoms can move together} to \emph{which bond changes those moves produce}.

\subsubsection{Local symmetry: retain independent and correlated moves}

For each endpoint of a fragment, compute symmetry on its own binary internal graph. Elements are preserved, anchors remain fixed, and bonds satisfying the presence criterion share one structural label. Original bond weights are retained for event counting. External attachments do not determine this local symmetry.

Nauty supplies compact generators~\citep{mckay2014}: unit operations that describe the allowed rearrangements. Exchanging two identical arms moves each arm with its attached atoms; exchanging two hydrogens can act within an arm. The two arms must still occupy different positions. These joint operations retain that correlation without storing every full permutation. Both endpoint actions are represented; for an aligned equal-connectivity pair, one side suffices to describe their relative rearrangements.

\subsubsection{Internal events: attach bond changes to the allowed moves}

Let $W_B$ and $W_T$ denote the original base and target bond-weight matrices. For mapped atoms $u,v$, an assignment $m$ changes the bond weight by
\begin{equation}
 \Delta W_{uv}(m)=W_T(m(u),m(v))-W_B(u,v).
 \label{eq:local-event}
\end{equation}
The pair contributes one event when $|\Delta W_{uv}|$ exceeds its event threshold. The sign distinguishes formation or strengthening from cleavage or weakening. These thresholds differ from the growth tolerance: a binary symmetry can preserve connectivity while changing bond weights.

In the acetate example (\Cref{fig:algorithm}c), exchanging the two oxygen images adds two internal events. The saved response associates the rearrangement with those changes, so every branch using that fragment can reuse the calculation. Independent symmetry components are processed separately when no event term or requested endpoint couples them. Event-invariant choices share computation; correlated choices remain joint. The response supports later event queries without expanding every complete mapping.

\subsubsection{Different connectivity: improve the starting correspondence}

The accepted growth graph can omit bonds present in the final fragment. In the substituted triangle--chain example (\Cref{fig:design-examples}b), the displayed local shuffles keep one internal ring-opening event while changing the outer attachment. More generally, two valid discovery correspondences on the same atom sets need not be connected by the final fragment symmetries. Optimizing only one starting correspondence can therefore miss a lower-event alternative.

MAPPA first improves the saved witness under local symmetry. Additional starting-correspondence search is allowed only when growth and final connectivity differ on at least one side and the witness has not attained an established event lower bound. Preparation and improvement share a fixed per-fragment budget. The output retains the selected starting correspondence and its symmetry response. An unsettled minimum is recorded as best found; selecting this starting orbit restricts decoded coverage. Solver details and bounds are given in \Cref{sec:decoding-details}.

\subsection{Decoding Stage: assemble branch events and select the window}
\label{sec:window}

A branch combines three contributions: internal fragment events, ordinary boundary events and the correction for the swept bond. For a branch $\mathcal B$ and a permitted correspondence $m$, the reported count is
\begin{equation}
 E_{\mathcal B}(m)=B_{\mathcal B}+\sum_i e_i(m)+s_{\mathcal B}(m),
 \qquad 0\leq s_{\mathcal B}(m)\leq 2.
 \label{eq:composition}
\end{equation}
Here $e_i$ is the reused internal response of a disjoint final fragment piece, $B_{\mathcal B}$ is the ordinary-boundary contribution, and $s_{\mathcal B}$ accounts for the swept connection. The following rules specify how they are combined.

\subsubsection{Ordinary boundaries: a fixed contribution under the model}

MAPPA uses the independent ordinary-boundary approximation: these contributions remain fixed as internal fragment choices change. Bonds touching mapped atoms are counted, including mapped/unmapped boundaries; bonds wholly within unmatched material are omitted. No assignment is invented for an unmatched atom.

Greedy growth helps absorb connections that would otherwise couple fragments. In C--C$\equiv$N $\rightarrow$ C--C--N (\Cref{fig:design-examples}c), tolerance one can leave C--C separate from N. Swapping the carbons changes the outer contribution from one weakening to a cleavage plus a formation. Tolerance two allows C--N inside the fragment, where the internal response handles that dependence. Greediness helps, but does not guarantee independence: the triangle--chain example still has a variable outer attachment. Equation~\eqref{eq:composition} is therefore a model count, which can differ from the physical whole-reaction count.

\subsubsection{The swept bond: reuse the two endpoint responses}

The single masked connection is handled separately. Its correction depends on where its two endpoints are placed, so assembly queries their jointly reachable positions in the already prepared fragment responses. It retains their correlation with internal events and one-to-one occupancy. It does not rebuild a whole-reaction bijection table. The swept contribution ranges from zero to two events.

\subsubsection{The event window: refine the best count, then retain nearby branches}

First establish the best attained branch count $U$. When attained local minima compose, the smallest pre-sweep composition $L$ can incur at most two additional sweep events. Only branch compositions that could improve this $L+2$ result need refinement. If local searches are unfinished or their minima cannot be combined, retain the lower bounds and an actual witness upper bound; do not assume $L+2$ is attainable.

Next retain branches that could contribute within $U+n$: a branch is excluded only when its lower bound exceeds that threshold. The studies here use $n=5$. The result stores the retained branches, shared fragment responses, event window and minimum/proof status. A later query requests a mapping at a selected count or with specified atom pairs, returning a witness, established absence or an unresolved outcome under its allowance. A retained count interval does not assert that every count inside it is attainable.

\subsection{Execution: parallel stages and shared computation}
\label{sec:reuse-execution}

The stage boundaries also define parallel work. Reactions and directions are independent; seed/cut searches within a direction can run concurrently before their results are reduced together. Growth along one branch remains sequential because later fragments depend on earlier occupancy. For the reported batch workflow, the matching stage, including reduction, finishes before catalogue decoding begins. Different reaction/direction catalogues can then be decoded independently, with unique-fragment responses shared within each catalogue.

A persistent worker pool schedules these operations through the same interfaces used by direct local calls. Intermediate records can remain in memory or use aggregated storage. Compatible fragment proofs can be reused during growth, but each seed and cut keeps its own random decisions. Matching and decoding use a shared symmetry core, and bulk operations use native kernels. Scheduling and persistence do not determine which mappings are allowed. Detailed solver, cache and storage protocols, together with full pseudocode, appear in \Cref{sec:algorithm-details}.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Datasets and search settings}
\label{sec:current-validation}

We evaluate all 1,851 Golden reactions~\citep{lin2022} and the prepared collection of 47,974 EnzymeMap reactions~\citep{heid2023enzymemap}. Reference labels are removed before search. Both studies use the prepared formal-bond-order matrices and explicit hydrogens; these results do not constitute a benchmark of continuously valued electronic-structure WBO inputs. EnzymeMap references are curated database mappings, so agreement is not independent validation of reaction mechanisms.

The common settings are discovery tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}=2$, presence/cut threshold $b=0.2$, hierarchical branch cap 100, random seed 42, both directions and the full single-edge sweep. Golden uses one and ten seed orderings per cut; EnzymeMap uses one. Decoding uses the original weights, thresholds 0.5/0.3, a five-second allowance per unique fragment and the best attained model count plus five. No reference mapping guides growth, pruning or starting-correspondence selection.

A separate paired Golden study evaluates current one-seed MAPPA and pinned SLAPMapper on identical prepared inputs and one CPU model. We distinguish standard binary SLAP, called on the uncut graph, from an expanded protocol combining binary and weighted modes, both directions and every single-edge cut. The latter is our exploration wrapper, not default SLAP or its published benchmark. Its comparison with default SLAP changes both search scope and weight mode; it is not a sweep-only ablation. Both methods retain their original algorithms, and external evaluation applies the same chemical endpoint-equivalence rule. The saved seed study and paired study have separately recorded runs and source identities; their recovery counts are not pooled.

\subsection{Catalogue and decoded-reference verification}
\label{sec:verification}

We report two external coverage measurements. \emph{Catalogue recovery} asks whether the reference belongs to the retained matching relation. \emph{Decoded recovery} requires an actual injective mapping from a saved starting correspondence and its joint symmetry choices, in a retained branch and event window. A positive certificate is replayed and its event count independently checked. A catalogue certificate may suggest a candidate, but cannot alone establish decoded membership.

The decoded audits preserve exact annotated heavy-atom indices and unmatched-heavy-atom requirements. Unannotated hydrogens must admit a feasible completion. Golden catalogue verification additionally permits endpoint chemical symmetries preserving charge, isotope, hydrogen count and stereochemistry; the Golden catalogue and decoded scores therefore differ in both representation and reference-equivalence policy. EnzymeMap uses exact indices for both. Recovery means inclusion in either search direction, not top-ranked prediction accuracy. Every reaction remains in the denominator, and unresolved queries remain separate from absence.

\subsection{Timing and branch-event analysis}
\label{sec:measurement}

Matching time includes both fragment discovery and reduction into the final catalogue; decoding is reported separately. CPU seconds sum successful worker computation over both directions and all cuts, including stage input loading and result construction. Publication, external verification, historical failed attempts and parallel elapsed duration have separate accounting. Timings use the saved successful passes and their documented recoveries, rather than a newly measured uninterrupted run of one final performance revision. Exact source and measurement boundaries are recorded in the repository.

To locate a recovered reference within the decoded event landscape, let $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{GT}}$ contain the retained branch contexts that admit it inside the saved window. We measure
\begin{equation}
 n_{\mathrm{GT}}=\min_{c\in\mathcal C_{\mathrm{GT}}}\min_{m\in c} E_c(m)-U,
 \label{eq:reference-offset}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the overall detected count, minimized across both directions. Ground truth selects the qualifying contexts; their event minima are queried without fixing the reference assignments. Both terms use the same saved all-atom decoder model. This measures where a GT-containing branch begins, not the physical event count of the reference itself. Missing references and unresolved offsets remain outside numeric bins.

We separately count the distinct heavy-atom mapping classes actually attainable at the detected minimum, taking the union across retained branches and both directions. Chemical endpoint equivalence identifies repeated representations, and hydrogen permutations do not multiply the choice count; each class must admit a full valid completion. The ranking score remains the saved all-atom model count. If a reaction has $K$ choices and its reference class is present, a uniform selector succeeds with probability $1/K$; otherwise it succeeds with probability zero. This defines expected random-choice accuracy without a trained selector. Incomplete counts contribute bounds, not exact values. A GT-containing branch's minimum need not be attained by GT itself, so this measurement is separate from \Cref{eq:reference-offset}.

\subsection{Seed count and computational scaling}

Increasing Golden from one to ten seeds increases matching CPU, including reduction, by 8.87-fold and decoding by 4.95-fold. Matching therefore scales approximately linearly with seed count in the measured one-to-ten-seed comparison. Decoding grows less because the extra searches produce a reduced set of shared fragments and branch compositions rather than ten independent decoding jobs. These measurements describe the cost of additional exploration and its coverage gain; they are not a controlled isolation of individual acceleration mechanisms.

\section{Discussion}

MAPPA separates the existence question needed during growth from the compressed alternatives needed at saturation, and then separates correspondence discovery from event interpretation. Atom-set reduction gives repeated fragments one shared identity. The decoder reuses local symmetry-event responses while retaining correlated occupancy and the special dependence introduced by a swept bond. These module boundaries make search, representation and interpretation independently inspectable and reusable.

The benchmarks demonstrate high reference inclusion with complete saved mapping and decoding outputs, while preserving the distinction between catalogue and decoded coverage. More Golden seeds recover additional references at increased computation, with matching cost approximately proportional to seed count over the measured range. The paired SLAP comparison shows the trade-off directly: default SLAP is cheaper, whereas MAPPA explores a broader alternative set and costs less than the expanded SLAP sweep. Returning alternatives is shared by both methods; the comparison concerns their coverage, organization and cost.

Several limits remain scientifically important. Greedy trajectories, sampled seeds, single-edge cuts and hierarchical caps restrict discovery. Selecting one original fragment record per atom-set pair and one decoded starting orbit can omit alternatives present in discarded discovery records. Bounded improvement can retain an unproved minimum. None of these restrictions establishes exhaustive correspondence recovery.

The fixed ordinary-boundary model is also an approximation. In the EnzymeMap audit, 2,801 positive directional witnesses have different model and physical whole-reaction event counts; the ten-seed Golden audit records 32 such witnesses. Both counts are retained. Model-window membership is therefore not physical event-bin accuracy, and a low model count alone does not identify the annotated or most plausible chemistry. The branch-offset figure assesses organization within that model, not a replacement accuracy measure.

Retained alternatives can support reaction-path preparation: atom-index assignment affects endpoint interpolation~\citep{de2019}, and different permitted correspondences could supply starting structures for nudged elastic band calculations~\citep{henkelman2000}. A separate chemical selector could turn the same catalogue into a single-answer prediction, using minimum event count as a surrogate and reaction-specific preferences to resolve ties or retain a higher-event candidate. These are downstream opportunities, not demonstrated improvements in ranking or transition-state prediction. The current Golden and EnzymeMap studies also do not validate continuously valued WBO inputs or every stereochemical attribute. The archived comparator, seed/cap, direction and coordinate analyses remain in the supplement with their original protocols; only the separately paired current comparison supports its stated speed ratios.
