\section{Introduction}

A chemical reaction connects two endpoints: the reactant structures ($R$) and the product structures ($P$), each of which may contain several molecules. Given these endpoints, atom-to-atom mapping (AAM) assigns corresponding atoms between $R$ and $P$, preserving their elements and using each mapped atom only once. This correspondence identifies which reactant atom becomes which product atom. Comparing the bonds between corresponding atom pairs then reveals the net bond changes: for example, a bond present in $R$ but absent in $P$ is a cleavage under that mapping. AAM therefore describes the endpoint result of chemical evolution; the intervening sequence of elementary steps requires additional information.

These assignments make reactions usable as structured data. They locate reaction centers and enable the extraction of transformation templates for reaction prediction and retrosynthesis~\citep{schwaller2021,chen2024}. They also establish atom identities between endpoint geometries for reaction-path calculations~\citep{de2019}. A mistaken correspondence can change the inferred transformation and propagate into downstream analyses. MAPPA addresses this task by exploring shared molecular fragments and the alternative mappings they support (\Cref{fig:algorithm}).

The difficulty is that endpoint structures alone may not select a unique correspondence. Repeated groups offer several possible matching sites, and molecular symmetry permits exchanges that leave a structure unchanged. Other assignments can imply different atom origins or different bond changes. For example, when several reactant molecules contain oxygen, deciding which oxygen appears in a product may require chemical knowledge beyond element identity. Favoring a small number of bond changes is a useful structural preference, but does not by itself establish the chemically correct atom history~\citep{chen2024}. A mapper must therefore balance the cost of exploring alternatives with the risk of discarding a useful one.

Golden case 9 makes this ambiguity concrete (\Cref{fig:alternatives}). The annotated correspondence places the alcohol oxygen in the product ring and the ketone oxygen in water. A second assignment exchanges those oxygen origins, while a third also changes two carbon assignments. The second has fewer bond events than the reference; the first and third have the same total but different atom origins. A minimum-event rule alone therefore does not select the annotation. Retaining alternatives leaves that chemical decision available to a subsequent selector.

Existing approaches use structural optimization or information learned from reaction data to guide these assignments. Structural methods seek shared substructures or favorable bond-change patterns~\citep{lin2022}; SLAPMapper uses iterative atom-label refinement and assignment optimization~\citep{koda2025}. RXNMapper obtains correspondence information from a reaction language model~\citep{schwaller2021}, while LocalMapper learns from chemist-labeled examples~\citep{chen2024}. For the present work, a further distinction is useful: whether the intended output is a set of alternatives or one selected prediction (\Cref{tab:aam-objectives}). This distinction concerns the answer supplied to the user and can apply to either structural or learned methods.

The two objectives can be combined in a discovery-and-selection workflow. An alternative-generating solver supplies candidates; a selector then chooses among them using a preference such as fewest bond events, chemical rules or a learned ranking. Coverage measures whether the reference remains available for that later choice, whereas top-1 accuracy measures whether the choice is correct. All methods can be compared by reference coverage: a single prediction is a candidate set containing one mapping. Coverage of a larger set does not establish equally high top-1 accuracy after selection. MAPPA focuses on the discovery step and retains alternatives for subsequent interpretation and selection.

The central challenge for this objective is to preserve useful diversity without repeatedly processing equivalent atom assignments. MAPPA treats mapping as a constraint-search problem: shared molecular fragments restrict which atoms can correspond, while compact symmetry relationships retain allowed rearrangements. The matching stage discovers these fragments and their combinations, then consolidates repeated fragments and branch compositions. The decoding stage relates the retained alternatives to bond changes. This separation allows fragment calculations to be reused across alternatives and supports parallel exploration. We evaluate reference coverage, computational cost and the choices remaining under a minimum-bond-event preference on Golden~\citep{lin2022} and EnzymeMap~\citep{heid2023enzymemap}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Overview: from shared fragments to alternative mappings}
\label{sec:architecture}
\label{sec:design-principle}

MAPPA builds atom mappings by growing structural fragments shared between reactants and products (\Cref{fig:algorithm}). Starting from a seed atom, it extends a fragment while a compatible placement remains possible. When no admissible extension remains, it finds the completed fragment's alternative placements. Each placement defines a search branch: that choice is locked, and fragment growth continues on the remaining atoms under the accumulated assignments. Repeating this process produces alternative compositions of matched fragments. Different seeds, single-bond cuts and search directions expose additional compositions.

Two stages organize this procedure. The \emph{matching stage} discovers fragments and their conditional placements, then reduces repeated fragments and branch compositions into a shared mapping catalogue. Reduction is its final subphase. The \emph{decoding stage} uses that catalogue to relate permitted atom rearrangements to bond events, preparing each unique fragment once and combining its response with the branch boundaries. Its output retains branches that can be queried within a chosen event-count window, rather than only one selected mapping. Matching and decoding are separate operations; a saved catalogue can be decoded without repeating discovery. Reference verification evaluates these outputs separately (\Cref{sec:verification}).

\subsection{Matching Stage: greedy fragment growth and hierarchical branching}
\label{sec:matching-rules}
\label{sec:growth}

Consider one search direction, with a source and a target molecular graph. Atoms carry element labels, and bonds carry their original weights; explicit hydrogens participate. A fragment begins at a source seed and grows through neighboring connections. Each proposed extension asks whether the enlarged fragment has at least one compatible, one-to-one placement in the target. An explicit valid assignment is called a \emph{witness}. A positive answer accepts the extension and updates that witness. An impossible extension is deferred while other frontier connections are explored. Growth stops when no admissible extension remains.

Compatibility requires equal elements, distinct target atoms, target bond presence and a source--target bond-weight difference within the discovery tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}$. The presence threshold $b$ determines which source connections can be explored. Growth selects a boundary atom uniformly, then an incident frontier edge uniformly; weights do not rank proposals. Only accepted growth edges become preservation constraints. Other bonds between the included atoms are not automatically required, and source nonedges impose no prohibition. Consequently, the accepted growth graph can differ from the completed fragment's full internal connectivity. Coordinates may accompany the input but do not determine growth.

Finding one witness is sufficient to decide whether growth can continue. The current assignment is extended when possible; otherwise MAPPA can rearrange assignments within the growing fragment or reuse a compatible previously discovered fragment. Assignments locked by earlier fragments remain fixed. If a search is needed, it stops at the first witness; only exhausted alternatives establish absence. A resource limit is recorded as incomplete computation. Joining a previously completed fragment retains its locked assignments and accepted constraints.

\label{sec:completion}
At saturation, the question changes from whether a match exists to where the completed fragment can be placed. MAPPA searches the full accepted constraints for distinct target atom sets, representing their internal alternatives compactly. For example, a six-carbon ring can occupy either of two target rings with distinct surrounding environments. These are two atom-set placements; rotations within either ring do not create additional placements. The search excludes already represented placements directly, avoiding enumeration of every internal bijection followed by merging.

Each retained placement conditions the next fragment search. If fragment A occupies one target site, fragment B must use the remaining compatible sites, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:algorithm}a. Whole-endpoint symmetry can sometimes connect different atom-set placements and allow their later searches to be shared. This is used only when the transformation preserves every subsequent matching constraint and earlier lock; it moves the continuation jointly. Otherwise the placements remain separate. The branch cap limits retained continuations through pruning from the leaves upward, using the reduced descendant counts at each level. Internal symmetry permutations do not count as separate branches.

This construction treats mapping as constraint discovery. A locked fragment expresses a structure that can coexist in both endpoints, restricting the choices left for subsequent fragments. Greedy growth extends that constraint as far as the current trajectory permits. The fragment is locally saturated, not necessarily the largest common fragment obtainable from every starting point. This motivates the exploration strategy below; precise matching conditions and hierarchy rules are given in \Cref{sec:algorithm-details}.

\subsection{Matching Stage: exploring seeds and single-bond cuts}
\label{sec:cut-sweep}

One greedy trajectory can repeatedly favor a large preserved region and miss another useful decomposition. In \Cref{fig:design-examples}a, growing the large B--A region first tends to leave R--B as the next fragment. If B--A contains 90\% of the eligible seed atoms, a uniformly chosen initial seed falls there with probability 0.9. Additional seeds can discover different constraints, but may also revisit the dominant decomposition.
\par\end{samepage}

A single-edge \emph{cut sweep} perturbs this commitment systematically. MAPPA searches the uncut graph and then repeats the search with each eligible source edge temporarily masked, including bonds to hydrogen. Each cut condition receives the requested number of seed orderings. Masking a connection inside the dominant region can expose a trajectory that ordinary seeding rarely reaches. The original endpoint weights remain unchanged for decoding: a mask alters discovery and does not assert a broken bond. Its endpoints may reconnect through another route, and one cut can redirect several later fragment choices.

Searches in both reaction directions add further alternatives. Seeds, cuts and reverse searches broaden discovery without making it exhaustive. The same exploration principle can be applied to another mapper; our expanded SLAP protocol reaches approximately 97\% coverage, although its changes in both search scope and weight mode prevent a sweep-only attribution (\Cref{sec:paired-slap}).

\subsection{Matching Stage: consolidating unique fragments and branch compositions}
\label{sec:reduction}

The final subphase of matching consolidates the search results. Because different searches often rediscover the same fragment, reduction identifies it by its source and target atom-set pair $(R_i,P_i)$ and gives that pair one shared record. For example, if two seed searches both match source atoms $\{1,2,3\}$ to target atoms $\{a,b,c\}$, they reference the same completed fragment even if their growth order or starting witness differs. Each branch becomes a composition of these shared fragment identities, allowing repeated compositions to be removed before decoding.

The retained record keeps one original witness, its discovery constraints, anchors and symmetry information intact. Duplicate records are discarded rather than combined into broader mapping permissions. Branches also retain their ordered placement context, because a shared placement transformation must move its continuation consistently. A \emph{mapping family} stores these retained local relations and placements with their alternative partitions and cut provenance. Different fragment partitions remain alternatives, not contributions to be added together.

Reduction reuses the fragment inventories accumulated during matching. It performs structural grouping and deduplication, without symmetry optimization or bond-event computation. Selecting one record per atom-set pair does not preserve the union of every discarded discovery relation; this restriction remains part of the method. The output is one catalogue per reaction and search direction, combining the requested seeds and cuts. That complete catalogue supplies the decoder.

\subsection{Decoding Stage: local fragment symmetry and internal bond events}
\label{sec:decoding}

Decoding first identifies the unique fragments and which branches use them. Its goal is to compute a fragment's local symmetry-to-bond-event relationship once, then reuse it across all those branches. A response records which rearrangements remain possible and how they change the internal event count. Branch assembly follows in \Cref{sec:window}.

Local symmetry is computed on each fragment's own binary internal graph, preserving elements and fixing its anchors. Bonds satisfying the presence criterion share one structural label; their original weights remain available for event counting. External attachments do not define the fragment's internal symmetry. Nauty supplies compact generators~\citep{mckay2014}, each describing a permitted joint rearrangement. For example, exchanging two identical arms moves each arm with its attached atoms, while an internal hydrogen exchange can act within one arm. The arm choices must still occupy different positions. These correlated operations represent the alternatives without storing every complete permutation. Both endpoint actions are retained; for an aligned equal-connectivity pair, one side suffices to describe their relative rearrangements.

Events are evaluated from the original bond-weight differences under the resulting correspondence. A change counts when its magnitude is strictly greater than the event threshold, with its sign distinguishing formation or strengthening from cleavage or weakening. Our studies use 0.5, or 0.3 for a pair involving a metal. Discovery tolerance and event thresholds therefore serve different purposes: a permitted binary rearrangement can preserve connectivity while changing weighted bond events. In the acetate example (\Cref{fig:algorithm}c), exchanging the two oxygen images adds two internal bond events even though the binary connectivity is preserved. The response separates independent components when no event term or requested endpoint couples them, and shares computations for event-invariant choices.

For each fragment, the decoder improves the saved starting correspondence under its local symmetries. The growth graph and final internal connectivity can nevertheless differ, as in the substituted carbon triangle and chain in \Cref{fig:design-examples}b. The displayed rearrangements preserve the one-event internal ring-opening cost while changing the outer attachment. More generally, useful starting correspondences need not be connected by local symmetry. Additional starting-correspondence search is therefore restricted to fragments with such a connectivity mismatch whose current witness remains above a valid lower bound. Preparation and improvement share a fixed per-fragment budget. The retained result consists of one selected starting correspondence and its queryable symmetry response; unless a valid bound or complete search certifies optimality, its minimum is best found. Selecting this starting orbit is an explicit coverage restriction.

\subsection{Decoding Stage: boundary events, sweep correction and event windows}
\label{sec:window}

A branch combines the prepared internal responses with events at its fragment boundaries. MAPPA aims to share local calculations rather than enumerate whole-reaction assignments. It uses the independent ordinary-boundary approximation: ordinary boundary contributions stay fixed, while the single swept connection retains its dependence on the two endpoint assignments. For a branch $\mathcal B$ and a permitted correspondence $m$, the reported count is
\begin{equation}
 E_{\mathcal B}(m)=B_{\mathcal B}+\sum_i e_i(m)+s_{\mathcal B}(m),
 \qquad 0\leq s_{\mathcal B}(m)\leq 2.
 \label{eq:composition}
\end{equation}
Here $e_i$ counts internal events owned by a disjoint final fragment piece, $B_{\mathcal B}$ is the ordinary-boundary contribution, and $s_{\mathcal B}$ is the swept bond's correction. Bonds touching mapped atoms are counted, including mapped/unmapped boundaries; bonds wholly inside unmatched material are omitted. No correspondence is invented for an unmatched atom. Assembly reuses the local responses and preserves shared choices, one-to-one occupancy and the correlation between the sweep endpoints and internal events.

Greedy growth helps make the boundary approximation useful by absorbing compatible connections before fragments lock. In C--C$\equiv$N $\rightarrow$ C--C--N (\Cref{fig:design-examples}c), tolerance one can leave C--C separate from N. Exchanging the two carbons then changes the outer contribution from one weakening to a cleavage plus a formation. Tolerance two allows that connection inside one fragment, where its dependence is handled by the internal response. Greediness reduces such artificial boundaries but cannot guarantee independence: the ring--chain example still has a variable outer attachment. Equation~\eqref{eq:composition} is consequently a model count, which may differ from the physical whole-reaction count.

The requested event window focuses decoding on low-event alternatives. Local bounds and actual branch witnesses first establish a best attained count $U$. When attained local minima compose, the smallest pre-sweep composition $L$ has an attainable upper bound no greater than $L+2$; only compositions that could improve the current best require further refinement. Unfinished local searches or incompatible local minima instead retain their lower bounds and an actual attained upper bound, without assuming that $L+2$ is attainable. Branches whose lower bounds exceed $U+n$ are excluded. The studies below use a slack of $n=5$.

The result retains branch identities, shared fragment responses, the event window and minimum/proof status. A later query can request a correspondence at a selected count or with specified atom pairs. It returns a concrete witness, established absence or an unresolved outcome under its allowance. Retaining a count interval does not assert that every count inside it is attainable. A proved model minimum, a best-found model count and a physical event minimum remain distinct.

\subsection{Execution: parallel stages and shared computation}
\label{sec:reuse-execution}

The stage boundaries also define parallel work. Reactions and directions are independent; seed/cut searches within a direction can run concurrently before their results are reduced together. Growth along one branch remains sequential because later fragments depend on earlier occupancy. For the reported batch workflow, the matching stage, including reduction, finishes before catalogue decoding begins. Different reaction/direction catalogues can then be decoded independently, with unique-fragment responses shared within each catalogue.

A persistent worker pool schedules these operations through the same interfaces used by direct local calls. Intermediate records can remain in memory or use aggregated storage. Compatible fragment proofs can be reused during growth, but each seed and cut keeps its own random decisions. Matching and decoding use a shared symmetry core, and bulk operations use native kernels. Scheduling and persistence do not determine which mappings are allowed. Detailed solver, cache and storage protocols, together with full pseudocode, appear in \Cref{sec:algorithm-details}.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Datasets and search settings}
\label{sec:current-validation}

We evaluate all 1,851 Golden reactions~\citep{lin2022} and the prepared collection of 47,974 EnzymeMap reactions~\citep{heid2023enzymemap}. Reference labels are removed before search. Both studies use the prepared formal-bond-order matrices and explicit hydrogens; these results do not constitute a benchmark of continuously valued electronic-structure WBO inputs. EnzymeMap references are curated database mappings, so agreement is not independent validation of reaction mechanisms.

The common settings are discovery tolerance $\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}=2$, presence/cut threshold $b=0.2$, hierarchical branch cap 100, random seed 42, both directions and the full single-edge sweep. Golden uses one and ten seed orderings per cut; EnzymeMap uses one. Decoding uses the original weights, thresholds 0.5/0.3, a five-second allowance per unique fragment and the best attained model count plus five. No reference mapping guides growth, pruning or starting-correspondence selection.

A separate paired Golden study evaluates current one-seed MAPPA and pinned SLAPMapper on identical prepared inputs and one CPU model. We distinguish standard binary SLAP, called on the uncut graph, from an expanded protocol combining binary and weighted modes, both directions and every single-edge cut. The latter is our exploration wrapper, not default SLAP or its published benchmark. Its comparison with default SLAP changes both search scope and weight mode; it is not a sweep-only ablation. Both methods retain their original algorithms, and external evaluation applies the same chemical endpoint-equivalence rule. The saved seed study and paired study have separately recorded runs and source identities; their recovery counts are not pooled.

\subsection{Catalogue and decoded-reference verification}
\label{sec:verification}

We report two external coverage measurements. \emph{Catalogue recovery} asks whether the reference belongs to the retained matching relation. \emph{Decoded recovery} requires an actual injective mapping from a saved starting correspondence and its joint symmetry choices, in a retained branch and event window. A positive certificate is replayed and its event count independently checked. A catalogue certificate may suggest a candidate, but cannot alone establish decoded membership.

The decoded audits preserve exact annotated heavy-atom indices and unmatched-heavy-atom requirements. Unannotated hydrogens must admit a feasible completion. Golden catalogue verification additionally permits endpoint chemical symmetries preserving charge, isotope, hydrogen count and stereochemistry; the Golden catalogue and decoded scores therefore differ in both representation and reference-equivalence policy. EnzymeMap uses exact indices for both. Recovery means inclusion in either search direction, not top-ranked prediction accuracy. Every reaction remains in the denominator, and unresolved queries remain separate from absence.

\subsection{Timing and branch-event analysis}
\label{sec:measurement}

Matching time includes both fragment discovery and reduction into the final catalogue; decoding is reported separately. CPU seconds sum successful worker computation over both directions and all cuts, including stage input loading and result construction. Publication, external verification, historical failed attempts and parallel elapsed duration have separate accounting. Timings use the saved successful passes and their documented recoveries, rather than a newly measured uninterrupted run of one final performance revision. Exact source and measurement boundaries are recorded in the repository.

To locate a recovered reference within the decoded event landscape, let $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{GT}}$ contain the retained branch contexts that admit it inside the saved window. We measure
\begin{equation}
 n_{\mathrm{GT}}=\min_{c\in\mathcal C_{\mathrm{GT}}}\min_{m\in c} E_c(m)-U,
 \label{eq:reference-offset}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the overall detected count, minimized across both directions. Ground truth selects the qualifying contexts; their event minima are queried without fixing the reference assignments. Both terms use the same saved all-atom decoder model. This measures where a GT-containing branch begins, not the physical event count of the reference itself. Missing references and unresolved offsets remain outside numeric bins.

We separately count the distinct heavy-atom mapping classes actually attainable at the detected minimum, taking the union across retained branches and both directions. Chemical endpoint equivalence identifies repeated representations, and hydrogen permutations do not multiply the choice count; each class must admit a full valid completion. The ranking score remains the saved all-atom model count. If a reaction has $K$ choices and its reference class is present, a uniform selector succeeds with probability $1/K$; otherwise it succeeds with probability zero. This defines expected random-choice accuracy without a trained selector. Incomplete counts contribute bounds, not exact values. A GT-containing branch's minimum need not be attained by GT itself, so this measurement is separate from \Cref{eq:reference-offset}.

\subsection{Seed count and computational scaling}

Increasing Golden from one to ten seeds increases matching CPU, including reduction, by 8.87-fold and decoding by 4.95-fold. Matching therefore scales approximately linearly with seed count in the measured one-to-ten-seed comparison. Decoding grows less because the extra searches produce a reduced set of shared fragments and branch compositions rather than ten independent decoding jobs. These measurements describe the cost of additional exploration and its coverage gain; they are not a controlled isolation of individual acceleration mechanisms.

The paired study recovers 1,839/1,851 references with MAPPA (99.352\%), 1,794 with expanded SLAP (96.921\%), and 1,620 with standard binary SLAP across both directions (87.520\%; \Cref{fig:paired-slap}). Expanded SLAP has 52 confirmed misses and five unresolved reactions; standard bidirectional SLAP has 230 misses and one unresolved. MAPPA has 12 misses and no unresolved catalogue queries. The independent comparator audit reconstructs its actual returned heavy mappings and repeats chemical-equivalence verification with a separate graph-matching implementation: all 3,683 completed expanded-SLAP direction verdicts agree.

Default forward-only SLAP recovers 1,591/1,851 (85.95\%). This is close to its original paper's 86.9\%, which also measures reference inclusion among alternatives but uses a 1,758-reaction Golden version~\citep{koda2025}. It is therefore an independent reproduction of similar behavior, not an exact replication of that dataset. Our archived first-returned default-SLAP score is 1,399/1,851 (75.58\%). The retained alternatives tie at SLAP's lowest found cost; their output order is not a separate confidence ranking. For the single-answer interfaces in \Cref{tab:method-coverage}, coverage is the accuracy of their selected prediction.

On the 1,834 reactions completed by all three protocols, mean search costs are 0.111 CPU seconds for standard binary SLAP, 1.202 for MAPPA, and 12.475 for expanded SLAP, summing both directions. MAPPA decoding adds 1.617 seconds. Thus MAPPA search is 10.38 times cheaper than the expanded SLAP sweep, but standard SLAP is 10.80 times cheaper than MAPPA search. The broader standard-SLAP/MAPPA pair contains 1,849 completed reactions and retains a slower standard-SLAP tail: their means are 0.487 and 1.259 seconds, a 2.58-fold difference. These are paired measurements on Intel Xeon Gold 6442Y processors, excluding loading, publication and external verification. Failed attempts remain separately accounted; excluded reactions are not treated as fast completions.

\subsection{How many choices remain at the minimum?}
\label{sec:minimum-choices}

The minimum-event rule produces a compact shortlist in the saved one-seed Golden study (\Cref{fig:minimum-choices}). Exact counts are established for 1,829 reactions; 22 remain incomplete under the counting allowance. Among the certified cases, 1,370 have one choice, 352 have two, 96 have three to five, and 11 have six or more. The certified-subset mean is 1.36 choices. Even allowing arbitrary larger counts for unfinished cases, the full-dataset median is one and its 95th percentile is three. We do not estimate the full-dataset mean from the completed subset.

The reference is confirmed at the detected minimum in 1,691 reactions, absent there in 158, and unresolved in two: minimum-set coverage is therefore 91.36--91.46\%. For example, Golden case 1694 has eight distinct minimum-event choices, including the reference, giving a random selector probability $1/8$. They do not establish top-1 superiority over another mapper, and a reference outside the minimum set would require selection from a wider event window.

\section{Discussion}

MAPPA separates the existence question needed during growth from the compressed alternatives needed at saturation, and then separates correspondence discovery from event interpretation. Atom-set reduction gives repeated fragments one shared identity. The decoder reuses local symmetry-event responses while retaining correlated occupancy and the special dependence introduced by a swept bond. These module boundaries make search, representation and interpretation independently inspectable and reusable.

The benchmarks demonstrate high reference inclusion with complete saved mapping and decoding outputs, while preserving the distinction between catalogue and decoded coverage. More Golden seeds recover additional references at increased computation, with matching cost approximately proportional to seed count over the measured range. The paired SLAP comparison shows the trade-off directly: default SLAP is cheaper, whereas MAPPA explores a broader alternative set and costs less than the expanded SLAP sweep. Returning alternatives is shared by both methods; the comparison concerns their coverage, organization and cost.

Several limits remain scientifically important. Greedy trajectories, sampled seeds, single-edge cuts and hierarchical caps restrict discovery. Selecting one original fragment record per atom-set pair and one decoded starting orbit can omit alternatives present in discarded discovery records. Bounded improvement can retain an unproved minimum. None of these restrictions establishes exhaustive correspondence recovery.

The fixed ordinary-boundary model is also an approximation. In the EnzymeMap audit, 2,801 positive directional witnesses have different model and physical whole-reaction event counts; the ten-seed Golden audit records 32 such witnesses. Both counts are retained. Model-window membership is therefore not physical event-bin accuracy, and a low model count alone does not identify the annotated or most plausible chemistry. The branch-offset figure assesses organization within that model, not a replacement accuracy measure.

Retained alternatives can support reaction-path preparation: atom-index assignment affects endpoint interpolation~\citep{de2019}, and different permitted correspondences could supply starting structures for nudged elastic band calculations~\citep{henkelman2000}. A separate chemical selector could turn the same catalogue into a single-answer prediction, using minimum event count as a surrogate and reaction-specific preferences to resolve ties or retain a higher-event candidate. These are downstream opportunities, not demonstrated improvements in ranking or transition-state prediction. The current Golden and EnzymeMap studies also do not validate continuously valued WBO inputs or every stereochemical attribute. The archived comparator, seed/cap, direction and coordinate analyses remain in the supplement with their original protocols; only the separately paired current comparison supports its stated speed ratios.
