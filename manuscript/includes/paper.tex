\section{Introduction}

Atom-to-atom mapping identifies corresponding atoms across a chemical reaction. These correspondences support reaction-center identification, template extraction and the preparation of structures for reaction-path calculations. Yet endpoint structures do not always determine a unique assignment. Different correspondences may imply different atom origins and bond changes, while molecular symmetry can produce many equivalent assignments. A useful mapping method should retain structural alternatives without repeatedly listing permutations of the same choice.

Existing approaches obtain correspondences from learned reaction representations or graph-based optimization. RXNMapper uses attention from a reaction language model~\citep{schwaller2021}, LocalMapper learns from chemist-labeled reactions~\citep{chen2024}, and SLAPMapper combines label refinement with sequential linear assignment~\citep{koda2025}. For methods returning alternatives, recovering a reference somewhere in the output is a different objective from predicting one correct top-ranked mapping. Symmetry also makes the number of explicit assignments a poor measure of the number of structural choices.

Weighted molecular graphs introduce another distinction. Two assignments can both satisfy a permissive bond-weight matching tolerance while producing different bond events at a finer threshold. Retaining one representative alone can therefore hide information needed for chemical interpretation. The search for correspondences and their interpretation as bond changes should remain separate.

MAPPA separates three decisions: whether a fragment can continue growing, which compressed placements its completed constraints permit, and what bond changes those placements imply. Growth retains one compatible assignment. At saturation, a separate operation constructs the distinct compressed fragment placements, which become conditional branches for mapping the remaining atoms. A cut sweep varies the growth conditions to expose alternatives excluded by greedy fragment commitment. The completed search is reduced to a catalogue of mapping families, and a separate decoder shares local bond-event calculations across its unique fragments. We describe these stages and evaluate reference recovery on the Golden benchmark~\citep{lin2022}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Overview and terminology}

MAPPA takes two molecular endpoints and returns mapping families, each representing a set of allowed atom correspondences. A \emph{witness} is one explicit assignment. During growth, it establishes that the current fragment has at least one match. A \emph{compressed placement} represents the allowed assignments of a completed fragment. A \emph{search branch} records conditional fragment choices while the remaining atoms are mapped. A final \emph{mapping family} retains the resulting assignments together with the constraints and symmetry relations that connect them.

\Cref{fig:algorithm} connects the scientific stages through molecular examples. Matching grows fragments and constructs their compressed placements under the requested seed and cut conditions. Finalization and reduction collect the saved families into a catalogue for each reaction and direction. Decoding then interprets these families through shared local bond responses, without repeating fragment growth.

\subsection{Endpoint graphs and matching rules}
\label{sec:matching-rules}

Let $V_R,V_P$ denote the source and target atom sets, $Z_R,Z_P$ their element labels, and $W_R,W_P$ their symmetric bond-weight matrices. A partial mapping $m:D\subseteq V_R\rightarrow V_P$ assigns distinct target atoms to the mapped source atoms and preserves their elements. Explicit hydrogen atoms participate. Coordinates may accompany the endpoints but do not determine fragment growth.

The active source graph contains edges with weight at least $b$. A growing fragment retains a set $A$ of accepted preservation edges. Its witness must satisfy
\begin{equation}
 Z_R(u)=Z_P(m(u)),\qquad
 W_P(m(u),m(v))\geq b,\qquad
 |W_R(u,v)-W_P(m(u),m(v))|\leq\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}
 \label{eq:match}
\end{equation}
for each $(u,v)\in A$, while respecting assignments locked by completed fragments. Only an accepted growth edge adds a preservation constraint. Other bonds between included source atoms are not implicitly required, and source nonedges impose no negative constraints. The full original matrices remain available for subsequent event interpretation.

\subsection{Growing a fragment with one witness}
\label{sec:growth}

Starting from a seed, MAPPA chooses a boundary atom uniformly and then one of its incident frontier edges uniformly. It proposes adding that edge and asks whether the enlarged fragment has a compatible injective assignment. Bond weight does not rank the proposals.

Whenever possible, the existing witness is extended by checking the new edge and newly joined locked atoms. Otherwise, an exact existence query permits earlier assignments within the growing fragment to change; assignments belonging to previously completed fragments remain fixed. The query removes destinations that cannot satisfy element, bond or distinct-atom requirements, then searches the remaining choices only until it finds one witness. The same query proves absence if all remaining choices are excluded. Detailed propagation and search rules are given in \Cref{sec:existence-details}.

A successful proposal accepts the edge and replaces the witness. Proven absence defers the proposal while other frontier edges are explored. A resource limit remains an unresolved search, rather than a negative match. Joining a completed fragment preserves its locked assignments and accepted constraints. Growth saturates when no further admissible proposal remains. Thus, intermediate growth needs one witness, regardless of how many assignments that fragment could ultimately represent.

\subsection{Constructing compressed placements at saturation}
\label{sec:completion}

At saturation, MAPPA uses the finished fragment constraints to construct its distinct compressed placements. Compression has two complementary forms. An interchangeable \emph{pool} stores target atoms among which every represented injective assignment is valid. A correlated symmetry action describes atoms that must move together. For example, a rotation of a ring changes several atom assignments jointly; allowing its atoms to move independently would introduce invalid combinations.

Completion allocates source atoms to interchangeable target classes and uses the exact symmetry of the globally colored target graph, conditioned on earlier locks and the matching context, to avoid constructing equivalent allocations. Each returned placement carries a witness and the compact choices it represents. Symmetric assignments within that placement remain compressed. Exact graph automorphisms are computed with nauty~\citep{mckay2014}; the class construction and conditioned symmetry action are specified in \Cref{sec:completion-details}.

Local interchangeability and global symmetry serve different purposes. A tolerance can admit several target bond weights without making those weights globally equivalent. Likewise, a symmetry action can move atoms outside the current fragment as well as those inside it. MAPPA retains the joint restrictions needed for later placements. Equal atom sets or individual symmetry labels alone do not justify treating all assignments as interchangeable.

\subsection{Conditional branching and cut-sweep exploration}
\label{sec:cut-sweep}

Each retained completed-fragment placement creates one continuation for the remaining atoms. Earlier placements condition later choices, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:algorithm}a. The branch cap counts compressed choices at each hierarchy level, including the combined live continuations across parents. Assignments represented inside a pool are not separate branches. Expansions exceeding the cap are recorded as truncated; a capped result does not establish exhaustive coverage.

Fragment growth is greedy along a trajectory: accepting an edge can exclude placements that would be available with a different fragment boundary. MAPPA explores alternative trajectories through seed orderings and a cut sweep. For a source graph $G_R=(V_R,E_R)$, the sweep searches the uncut graph and one graph $G_R^{(e)}=(V_R,E_R\setminus\{e\})$ for each eligible edge $e$. At the evaluated settings, the cut threshold equals the active-graph floor, so the sweep includes $|E_R|+1$ conditions, including cuts involving H.

Masking an edge changes which preservation constraints growth can acquire. It can redirect growth, expose another placement or leave atoms for a later fragment (\Cref{fig:algorithm}b). The endpoints of the masked edge may still join through another route, and their final correspondence may preserve the bond. A cut is therefore a search condition, not a prescribed bond event. One cut can redirect several subsequent placements and expose a correspondence with multiple bond changes. Combining both search directions further broadens the explored choices, while retaining each family's orientation. The sweep and seed orderings improve exploration without making greedy search exhaustive.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{MAPPA matching and catalogue construction}
\label{alg:mappa-overview}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Endpoint graphs, matching policies, seed/cut plan and search budgets
\ENSURE A mapping-family catalogue for each search direction
\FORALL{requested directions}
  \STATE Initialize collected search results
  \FORALL{cut conditions and seed orderings}
    \STATE Initialize live branches with any supplied anchors
    \WHILE{a live branch can continue under the seed ordering}
      \STATE Select an eligible branch and fragment seed
      \STATE Grow with one-witness existence queries until saturation
      \IF{an admissible fragment has been established}
        \STATE Construct distinct compressed placements of the finished fragment
        \STATE Continue each retained placement with completed assignments locked
      \ELSE
        \STATE Retain the branch for later seeds
      \ENDIF
      \STATE Enforce hierarchy caps and record completion, stalls or truncation
    \ENDWHILE
    \STATE Retain terminal results and their provenance
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE Finalize saved symmetry; group fragment pairs and reduce repeated families
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Aggregating the final mapping families}
\label{sec:reduction}

After matching, saved symmetry is finalized and results from all supplied seeds and cuts are collected for each reaction and direction. Final branches are grouped by the unordered collection of their matched fragment atom-set pairs,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal B=\{(R_1,P_1),\ldots,(R_q,P_q)\}.
 \label{eq:branch}
\end{equation}
Growing fragment A before B or B before A does not create a new final group when the paired sets agree. Each group nevertheless retains its mapping families: different preserved edges or correlated actions can permit different assignments even when the fragment sets coincide.

Reduction shares identical normalized family records while preserving distinct constraints, alternative fragment partitions and provenance. It preserves the union of represented mappings and does not assume that the remaining families are mutually disjoint. This cross-search reduction is separate from constructing compressed placements during matching. The resulting catalogue is the input to decoding; a single cut is not an independent decoding unit.

\subsection{Decoding through shared local bond responses}
\label{sec:decoding}

Decoding translates the saved mapping choices into bond changes using the original endpoint matrices. For mapped atoms $u,v$, the signed change is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{uv}(m)=W_P(m(u),m(v))-W_R(u,v).
 \label{eq:events}
\end{equation}
An event occurs when $|\Delta_{uv}|$ is strictly greater than its threshold: $0.3$ for a pair involving a metal and $0.5$ otherwise. Positive changes indicate formation or strengthening; negative changes indicate cleavage or weakening. These thresholds are independent of the matching tolerance. Matching-compatible assignments can consequently produce different events (\Cref{fig:algorithm}c).

The decoder first collects all unique saved fragments across the complete catalogue, together with their family occurrences, constraints, symmetry actions and boundary relationships. Only after this inventory is complete does it calculate reusable local responses. The responses cover bonds within fragments, between fragments and across mapped/unmatched boundaries. Bonds wholly inside unmatched material are omitted; no correspondence is invented for unmatched atoms.

Each bond response depends on the destinations of its two endpoints. If the response is constant over the allowed destinations, it can be recorded directly. Otherwise, the decoder follows the reachable endpoint pairs under the saved actions, avoiding enumeration of the full molecular assignment family. Responses are shared only when their relevant fragment and constraint context agree. \Cref{sec:decoding-details} gives the construction.

For a requested joint choice, the decoder checks the full family constraints and combines the local contributions, counting each bond once. An action shared across several fragments remains one correlated choice. Alternative fragment partitions are alternative descriptions of the same family, so their totals are not added together. Ordinary decoding retains these local responses and composition rules; enumerating whole-reaction event patterns or selecting a minimum-event mapping are separate queries.

Matching, family reduction and decoding have separate interfaces for local and distributed execution. Completed-fragment results can be reused when their recorded dependencies remain valid; cache eviction changes computation, not the scientific result. Technical cache and execution details are given in \Cref{sec:cache-details}.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Reference verification}
\label{sec:verification}

We evaluate MAPPA with a separate verifier that asks whether an annotated atom relation belongs to the retained mapping catalogue under a declared atom subset and endpoint-equivalence policy. The verifier queries the compressed relation rather than comparing only its representative witness; it is not part of the mapping or decoding algorithm. An unresolved query is reported separately from verified inclusion or exclusion. A reference absent from the saved catalogue may still be compatible with search choices that were not explored.

\subsection{Golden reference recovery}
\label{sec:current-validation}

We evaluated the one-witness/direct-completion design on all \CurrentGoldenTotal{} Golden reactions~\citep{lin2022}, comprising \CurrentGoldenDirections{} search directions. Inputs use formal bond orders and explicit H, with reference labels removed before search. The run used one seed ordering per cut, random seed 42, branch cap 100, both directions, the full cut sweep, $b=0.2$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{iso}}=1$. Event thresholds were $0.5$, or $0.3$ for pairs involving metals.

Recovery requires the annotated heavy-atom relation, including unmatched atoms, modulo endpoint chemical symmetries preserving charge, isotope, H count and stereochemistry. These attributes define reference equivalence; they do not imply that element/bond-weight matching itself enforces every chemical attribute. A reaction is recovered if either direction contains its reference. This measures inclusion among alternatives, not top-ranked prediction accuracy. Failures and unresolved cases remain in the denominator.

MAPPA recovered \CurrentGoldenRecovered{} of \CurrentGoldenTotal{} reactions (\CurrentGoldenPercent\%) and \CurrentGoldenDirectionRecovered{} directional references (\Cref{fig:current-golden}). Matching, reduction, local decoding and verification completed for every direction, with no failed tasks or unknown verification outcomes. Twenty-one reaction references were absent from the retained families. Their absence is an outcome of the specified bounded search, not a proof that no compatible mapping exists.

These results apply to the recorded completed run. Its source version, per-case outcomes and measurement boundaries are provided in \Cref{sec:reproducibility}; later implementation revisions are not implicitly certified by this evaluation. Historical seed, cap and comparator studies are reported separately in \Cref{sec:historical-evaluation}.

\subsection{Computational cost}

\Cref{fig:current-golden} separates the measured costs of matching, family reduction, local decoding and reference verification. Summed worker CPU over both directions averaged 10.01 seconds per reaction across all four stages. This includes task computation and publication, but is not elapsed latency or total allocation CPU: coordinator activity, startup and idle capacity have different measurement boundaries. The run completed 177,552 matching tasks. Completed-fragment reuse answered 91.8\% of recorded fragment calls; this measures cache reuse, not a causal speedup against an uncached control.

The stage separation exposes both search and interpretation cost. In particular, fast matching does not imply negligible decoding: local response tables still depend on the number of reachable endpoint pairs and retained family contexts. The reported costs characterize this completed run and are not combined with historical timings from different hardware or scientific settings. Full stage measurements and their sampling coverage accompany the per-case evidence.

\subsection{Chemical interpretation of retained alternatives}

A correspondence describes net atom origins and bond changes without specifying intermediates or elementary steps. \Cref{fig:alternatives} illustrates why retaining alternatives can matter. In Golden case 9, the annotated assignment places the alcohol oxygen in the product ring and the ketone oxygen in water. A second assignment exchanges those oxygen origins, while a third also changes two carbon assignments. The second has fewer events than the reference; the first and third have equal event totals but different correspondences. Neither a minimum event count nor a single total identifies the annotated transformation.

The figure uses selected archived witnesses as a chemical illustration. It is not an exhaustive output of the current decoder, and the alternatives are not validated reaction pathways. The historical coordinate/WBO study in \Cref{sec:historical-evaluation} similarly examines event alternatives under unverified mappings; it is not a mapping-accuracy benchmark.

\section{Discussion}

MAPPA separates the decision needed during growth---whether one match exists---from the complete compressed representation needed when a fragment is committed. The final catalogue preserves structural alternatives and their correlated assignments, while local decoding reuses bond-response calculations across shared fragments. This makes correspondence search and chemical interpretation independently inspectable.

The method retains several limits. Exact fragment queries can still require substantial search on difficult inputs. Greedy growth, sampled seed orders, single-edge cuts and branch caps restrict structural coverage. Lossless representation of retained choices therefore does not imply exhaustive discovery of all mappings. Similarly, sharing local responses does not guarantee small tables for every family. Chemical conclusions depend on the input attributes, bond-weight model and event thresholds; a compatible correspondence is not evidence of a feasible mechanism.

Retained alternatives can support reaction-path preparation. Atom-index assignment affects endpoint interpolation~\citep{de2019}, and different correspondences or joint symmetry choices could supply starting structures for nudged elastic band calculations~\citep{henkelman2000}. Local bond events can also identify alternative reaction cores for mechanistic assessment. These are downstream uses of the representation; the present evaluation does not measure improvements in transition-state searches or pathway prediction.

The completed Golden study establishes reference-family recovery under its stated protocol. Broader annotated datasets and matched performance comparisons are needed to assess generalization and scaling. Historical studies remain available with their original scope in the supplement; they are not substituted for measurements of the present implementation.
